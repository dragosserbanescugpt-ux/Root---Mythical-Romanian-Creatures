20 beings between worlds

Encyclopedic entries based exclusively on Tudor Pamfile and Marcel Olinescu.

01

IeleleThe Enchanted Maidens

Supernatural female beings; spirits of the air and of natural spaces.

Description. The Iele are one of the most widespread and complex categories of female beings in Romanian folk mythology. In Marcel Olinescu’s synthesis, they are described as apparitions of unusual beauty, without an ordinary human body, but rather as “phantoms” in the form of young women. They are associated with night, moonlight, the air, forest clearings, orchards and isolated places. Their nature is ambivalent: their beauty, song and dance make them fascinating, but approaching them or breaking the taboos connected with them can have serious consequences.

Appearance and manifestation. They may appear naked or dressed in transparent garments, sometimes with chain mail on their chests and small bells at their feet. They travel through the air before midnight and are sometimes perceived from afar as lights passing through the sky. On moonlit nights they gather in clearings or orchards and dance the hora; the place of their dance may remain the next day as a circle of scorched grass or bare ground.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Their song has the power to enchant, while their hora can disturb the mind of whoever watches it. The sources attribute to them the punishment of those who disturb them, step on their dancing place, drink from water they have used, or break certain prohibitions. The effects are described through traditional ideas of “pocire” — supernatural affliction or disfigurement — as well as loss of speech, loss of reason or weakening of the body. Olinescu identifies Doamna Irodia as the leader of the Iele.

Associated beliefs and practices. Garlic and certain plants considered apotropaic appear especially in protective beliefs. Olinescu also mentions practices through which people try to avoid the effects of places where the Iele have passed or danced. Accounts of their origin vary; one legend recorded by Olinescu connects them with nine girls who drank living water and acquired supernatural powers.

Alternative names / analogies: Recorded names include Zânele, Rusaliile, Nemilostivele, Dânsele, Drăgaicele, Vântoasele, Vâlvele, Irodiencele, Frumoasele, Domnițele, Sfintele, Șoimanele and Fetele. The sources used do not indicate a single external equivalent; the many names instead point to a Romanian family of partially overlapping supernatural representations.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească. For regional variations and related beliefs: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

02

UrsitoareleThe Fates

Beings of destiny; fairies associated with birth, fate and the length of life.

Description. The Ursitoare are supernatural female beings who determine a child’s destiny shortly after birth. Pamfile and Olinescu most often present them as three in number, although regional variations are also recorded. Their decree determines the length of life, fortune, occupation, major events and manner of death. Their decision is considered extremely difficult or impossible to change, and in many versions the word of the third Ursitoare is decisive.

Appearance and manifestation. The sources do not establish a single appearance. They appear as maidens or fairies and come at night to the home of the newborn. Olinescu places them around the cradle and connects them with the symbolism of spinning: the eldest Ursitoare holds the distaff and spindle, Soartea spins the thread of life, while Moartea cuts it. In other traditions, they guard countless lamps in a palace, each lamp representing the life of one person.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Their main power is determining fate. In the model of the lamps of life, the amount of oil corresponds to the duration of existence. In other versions, destiny is written in the “book of life” or “book of fate.” Deliberately listening to the fate being pronounced is considered dangerous; some beliefs even predict a sudden death for anyone who overhears the words of the Ursitoare.

Associated beliefs and practices. During the first days after birth, gifts and a meal were prepared for the Ursitoare: bread, salt, water, wheat, oil, wine, candles or other objects, depending on the region. The meal was meant to welcome and appease them. The time of their arrival differs regionally, but the third night frequently appears in collected accounts.

Alternative names / analogies: Names recorded by Pamfile include Ursitori, Ursători, Ursite, Ursoi, Ursoaice and Ursoni; among the Macedo-Romanians, Mire and other regional names appear. Pamfile explicitly compares the Ursitoare with the Roman Parcae and the Greek Moirai: Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos form the closest classical analogy.

Source: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului; Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

03

Muma PăduriiMother of the Forest

Female forest spirit; a hostile and protective personification of the woodland.

Description. Muma Pădurii is one of the central figures associated with the forest in folk belief. Olinescu describes her as a spirit of the woods who rules over and guards the trees, but who can become dangerous to people who enter her domain. The figure combines two seemingly opposite functions: she cares for the trees and, at the same time, is an apparition used to explain fear, getting lost, illness or misfortune attributed to the forest.

Appearance and manifestation. She has the face of a very ugly old woman, tall and hunched, compared to a gnarled tree full of dry branches. Her hair falls in strands likened to snakes, and she walks through the forest leaning on a staff. Her dwelling is placed in a hut in the heart of the woods; she may also shelter in tree hollows.

Attributes / behavior / powers. She knows and tends the trees of the forest, raising, naming and punishing them. Toward people, she can frighten them into “pocire,” steal children’s sleep and, in some stories, abduct children left unattended. Olinescu also attributes to her the ability to influence the weather or send forest animals against a household.

Associated beliefs and practices. Protection is sought through the sign of the cross and through apotropaic objects or substances. For children, a broom, scissors, a poker or tongs placed crosswise are mentioned, as well as silver, incense, salt and garlic. Illnesses attributed to Muma Pădurii are treated through charms.

Alternative names / analogies: Pamfile’s material also includes formulas such as Surata din pădure, while Olinescu associates her with a male counterpart, Moșul Codrului. The sources used do not indicate a direct equivalent in another mythology.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

04

Zânele BuneThe Good Fairies

Benevolent female beings; protectors and helpers of humans.

Description. In the section devoted to them, Olinescu separates the Zânele Bune from dangerous female beings and presents them as beautiful supernatural maidens with a protective role. They appear as spirits of distinct natural domains: flowers, forests, clearings, streams, birds or lakes. Above them all there is said to be a Zână a Zânelor, described as possessing a beauty impossible to gaze upon.

Appearance and manifestation. They are imagined as young women of exceptional beauty and live in crystal palaces and paradisiacal gardens. In fairy tales they can intervene directly in the human world and, sometimes, may marry the hero, becoming like mortal women.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Their function is mainly beneficial: they help those who fight for justice, beauty or love and can bring good fortune to the household that receives them. Opposed to them are the zmei, presented by Olinescu as enemies of the fairies and abductors of beautiful beings.

Associated beliefs and practices. Olinescu records a Holy Week custom in which fires are lit in courtyards and water, unleavened bread, incense and chairs are prepared. The ritual is performed by children, considered pure, and the Zâne are said to come to wash, eat and warm themselves; wherever they come, they bring good fortune.

Alternative names / analogies: The term “zână” has a broad use in folklore and can also designate other female beings; here, however, Olinescu explicitly uses the category “Zânele Bune.” They should not automatically be equated with the Iele, even though “Zânele” also appears among the names of the latter. The sources used do not provide a direct external equivalent.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; for traditions concerning benevolent fairies: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

05

BrehneleThe Brehne Spirits

Small forest spirits; grotesque and mischievous beings.

Description. The Brehne occupy a more modest place in the Romanian bestiary than the Iele or Strigoi. Olinescu describes them as small spirits hidden in tree hollows and bushes, primarily concerned with frightening fearful people. They are not presented as beings with great destructive power and, in the author’s wording, “do no harm to anyone.”

Appearance and manifestation. Their defining feature is their grotesque and composite appearance. Their bodies may combine limbs and traits from animals, birds and insects. Olinescu’s example is a being with a frog’s head, a bird’s body and a lizard’s claws. There is therefore no single anatomy for the Brehne.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Their main power is to create fear through their appearance and behavior. They are beings of mischief and fright rather than demons intent on destroying people. This limitation is precisely what separates them from spirits with punitive or disease-causing functions.

Associated beliefs and practices. The consulted sources do not develop complex protective rituals against them, nor a cult of their own. Their role is mainly to populate the dark and hard-to-reach areas of the forest with strange presences.

Alternative names / analogies: Olinescu compares them directly in his definition with “mischievous spiriduși.” This is an internal analogy within the folklore described by the author, not an identity with the Spiriduș presented separately in the same work. No direct external equivalent is indicated.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

06

Știma ApeiThe Water Spirit

Female spirit of the waters; a dangerous aquatic being.

Description. Știma Apei is presented by Olinescu as the “living being of every water,” meaning the particular spirit of a river, stream, lake or body of still water. She personifies the independent and dangerous force of water. She is not a gentle protector, but a presence capable of demanding human lives and turning flooding into a deliberate action of the spirit.

Appearance and manifestation. On land she may appear as a white, tall, strong and proud woman, wearing a traditional shirt with an altiță and a headcloth. Her hair is very long and described as shining like gold. In water, the image changes: she is half woman and half fish.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Every body of water is said to have its own știmă. In Olinescu’s account, she may periodically demand a “human head”; when she does not receive her victim, the water may churn or overflow. During drought, Știma emerges near midnight and calls her victim. The person “destined” for her is compulsively drawn toward the water and dies upon entering it.

Associated beliefs and practices. The belief also functions as a mythical explanation for drowning, floods and the unpredictable character of waters. Some rivers are described as “howling” or becoming turbulent when they have not received their victim.

Alternative names / analogies: Olinescu explicitly calls her the “nymph of Romanian waters,” but specifies that she is a malevolent nymph, “closer to the sirens.” These are the external analogies offered by the source itself. The basic name remains Știma Apei / Știma Lacului.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; related traditions about water spirits also appear in Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

07

Șarpele CaseiThe Guardian House Snake

A protective household spirit embodied as a snake.

Description. Șarpele Casei is one of the few beings in the folk bestiary with a predominantly beneficial role. It represents good fortune, continuity and protection of the household. Olinescu states that every house has its own snake, while some beliefs recorded by Pamfile extend the connection to each member of the family.

Appearance and manifestation. It looks like an ordinary snake, although it is often described as white, a color explained by its life in the darkness of the walls. It lives inside walls and can produce a regular ticking sound, compared with the mechanism of a clock. It is gentle, does not bite and, in some accounts, drinks milk from the same bowl as children.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Its presence guarantees good fortune and protects the house from evil or magic. Killing it is considered a serious violation and may bring the death of a family member, the loss of good fortune or other dangers. The snake’s departure may foretell the abandonment of the house or someone’s death.

Associated beliefs and practices. The essential rule is that it must not be killed or driven away. In some versions, the ticking of the “clock” in the walls also takes on an omen-like meaning. Pamfile records similar beliefs in several Romanian regions and among Macedo-Romanians.

Alternative names / analogies: Șarpele Casei, Știma Casei, Ceasornicul de Casă. The sources do not identify an equivalent external deity, but Pamfile shows that this domestic belief also exists in Macedo-Romanian communities.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

08

SamcaThe Childbed Demon

A demonic female spirit associated with pregnancy, childbirth and early childhood.

Description. Samca concentrates traditional fears surrounding pregnancy, childbirth and the vulnerability of the newborn. Olinescu also calls her Avestița, the “wing of Satan,” and presents her as a being who intensifies labor pains, can kill women and can make children ill. The illness attributed to her action is itself called “samcă” or “spasm.”

Appearance and manifestation. She is described as a terrifying woman with sparse but very long hair, eyes red as fire, and distorted hands and body. Her body twists continuously and her speech is an unintelligible mumble. She is not limited to this form: she may appear as a hound, cat, shadow, locust, spider, fly and in other visible or invisible forms.

Attributes / behavior / powers. She attacks during sleep, especially pregnant women and children. Her changing nature allows her to enter houses in different forms. In the religious narrative reproduced by Olinescu, she is confronted by the Archangel Michael and forced to reveal her names and ways of acting.

Associated beliefs and practices. Protection is connected with the text of a prayer in which Samca’s nineteen names are written. The belief holds that wherever these names are kept, the being cannot approach. Charms for the illness attributed to her are also recorded.

Alternative names / analogies: Avestița, Aripa Satanei, Baba-Coaja; the prayer reproduced by Olinescu contains many additional ritual names. The sources used do not indicate a direct external equivalent.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

09

Ceasul cel RăuThe Evil Hour

A nocturnal spirit and personification of a dangerous interval of time.

Description. Ceasul cel Rău shows how folklore can transform time itself into a dangerous presence. Olinescu defines it as “a kind of spirit” that wanders at night and attacks those encountered late on lonely roads. It is not connected to one fixed appearance, but primarily to the moment and signs of its presence.

Appearance and manifestation. The sources do not provide a stable anatomy. The Ceasurile Rele are conceived as spirits moving through the air. Their approach could supposedly be recognized by a ringing in the ears, interpreted as a sign that such an unseen presence is passing nearby.

Attributes / behavior / powers. It can “poci” a person, meaning that it can cause a physical or spiritual disturbance attributed to a supernatural encounter. Olinescu speaks of three evil hours during the night. Danger is therefore connected with particular moments when travelling alone outside the home becomes risky.

Associated beliefs and practices. A quick protective gesture is to make the sign of the cross at the ear when the ringing occurs. If a person has already been touched by Ceasul cel Rău, healing is sought through a charm that sends the spirit far away, into the mountains and back to its own realm.

Alternative names / analogies: Olinescu uses the singular Ceasul cel Rău and the plural Ceasurile Rele. In other folkloric contexts the term may overlap with other nocturnal spirits, but the sources used do not provide a direct and stable external equivalent.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; for terminological overlaps with other nocturnal spirits: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

10

StrigoiulThe Revenant

Spirit of the dead and of people considered supernatural even during life.

Description. The Strigoi represents one of the most complex categories in Romanian folk demonology. The sources distinguish between dead strigoi, who leave the grave and return among the living, and living strigoi, people whose souls are believed to leave their bodies at night. This double nature explains the great variety of beliefs connected with birth, death, animals, weather, illness and household protection.

Appearance and manifestation. It has no single form. Olinescu records bodily signs of strigoi and the possibility of transforming into horses or other animals. Pamfile notes forms such as kids, cats, dogs, piglets, rats, hounds or wolves. In some regions, strigoi embodied as animals are called Pricolici.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Strigoi can cast the evil eye, take the productivity of cattle or fields, disturb the weather and harm relatives. The dead strigoi can grow stronger by sucking the blood of the living, especially those close to them. In some traditions, strigoi gather at night at boundaries, in cemeteries or abandoned places and fight among themselves.

Associated beliefs and practices. Garlic is one of the most important forms of protection and is used on doors, windows and around the household, especially on nights associated with strigoi. Special funerary practices are recorded for the suspected dead, while cases considered dangerous include exhumation and piercing of the corpse.

Alternative names / analogies: Strigoi, Strigoaică, Moroi/Moroaică; regional traditions include overlaps with the Pricolici and Vârcolac. Pamfile explicitly mentions the term Vampir among Megleno-Romanians and discusses parallels with the Balkan vampire. The vampire is therefore the clearest external analogy indicated by the source, without the terms being perfectly identical.

Source: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului; Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

11

VârcolaculThe Werewolf

A supernatural being or soul associated with the Moon, eclipses and animal transformation.

Description. The Vârcolac of Romanian traditions cannot be reduced to the modern image of the werewolf. Pamfile records several layers of belief: living people whose souls leave their bodies during sleep, beings capable of taking animal form and, above all, attackers of the Moon. Eclipses and the coppery color of the lunar disc are explained through the action of the Vârcolac that “eats” the Moon.

Appearance and manifestation. In some descriptions, a human vârcolac can be recognized by a pale, dry face and by an unusually deep sleep. The form of the soul that departs toward the Moon is not fixed. In other traditions, the vocabulary surrounding the vârcolac approaches the wolf or other animal forms.

Attributes / behavior / powers. During sleep, the soul may set out toward the Moon in order to devour it. If the sleeping body is moved, the soul is said to be unable to find the place through which it left, and the person may remain dead. The attack on the Moon explains eclipses and its reddish appearance, metaphorically interpreted as the blood of the celestial body.

Associated beliefs and practices. During eclipses, the community’s response may include making noise, striking objects or taking other actions intended to drive away the lunar attacker. Beliefs about the Vârcolac intersect with those about the Strigoi and Pricolici, which explains the variation in terminology.

Alternative names / analogies: Vârcolac/Vârcolaci; in some traditions it overlaps with the Pricolici or with animal forms of the Strigoi. Pamfile explicitly cites analogies with “Werwolf” and “loup-garou” for the meaning of werewolf and also notes Balkan identifications with the vampire or moroi. Nevertheless, the function of Moon-devourer remains essential in the tradition described.

Source: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului; Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

12

StafiaThe Ghost

Spirit of the dead tied to a place, a building or a violent death.

Description. Stafia is presented by Pamfile as the soul of a dead person that remains tied to a particular space. Unlike the Strigoi, whose activity may be more mobile and complex, Stafia is defined primarily through its relationship with a place: a building, a well, a cellar, a road, an inn, a mill or the site of a violent death.

Appearance and manifestation. It may appear in the likeness of the dead person, especially around midnight. There is no universal bodily description; what matters is the recognition of a human apparition or a “shadow” that repeats the presence of the departed and frightens travelers.

Attributes / behavior / powers. In one category of beliefs, Stafia becomes the guardian of a building and protects it from other evil spirits. In another, those who died violent deaths — by hanging, shooting, lightning or accidents — may become ghosts that appear at the place of death and block the path of those travelling late.

Associated beliefs and practices. Pamfile connects Stafia with the old belief of “building in the shadow”: a person’s shadow would be symbolically measured and incorporated into a construction to ensure its durability, and the soul of the person thus bound would become the guardian of the building. The author compares the motif with foundation-sacrifice traditions among several European peoples.

Alternative names / analogies: Stafie, Umbră; among Macedo-Romanians, Fantasma. Pamfile discusses parallels for foundation sacrifice and the protective spirit of a building among Greeks, Bulgarians, Serbs, Hungarians, Montenegrins, Albanians, Germans and Russians. These are analogies of folkloric motif, not identities between figures.

Source: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului; Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

13

ZburătorulThe Flyer

Nocturnal erotic spirit; personification of desire and tormenting love.

Description. Zburătorul is the spirit associated with erotic desire, impossible love or attraction that turns into suffering. Olinescu defines it as the “spirit of forbidden love” and the “spirit of carnal love.” Through supernatural intervention, it explains disturbing dreams, restlessness, weakness and states of illness in a person who is in love.

Appearance and manifestation. It is one of the beings with the most unstable form. It may take the appearance of the desired person, the form of a handsome young man, a snake or a scaly balaur. This ability to transform allows it to enter the house and approach the sleeping person.

Attributes / behavior / powers. It visits at night, causes dreams of love and leaves the person exhausted, with physical and emotional disturbances. In Olinescu’s descriptions, it may pinch, bite, kiss and torment the victim. The condition attributed to it is called “lipitură” or even “Zburătorul.”

Associated beliefs and practices. Healing is carried out through charms and through the use of plants and untouched water within a ritual practice. Zburătorul thus belongs to the domain of folk magical medicine as well as to the erotic imagination.

Alternative names / analogies: Zburătorul, Sburătorul; Pamfile treats it in close connection with Smeul and also records the name Al-mai-rău in certain traditions. Olinescu shows that Zmeul, in a second meaning, may be confused with Zburătorul. The sources used do not establish a direct external equivalent.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

14

ZmeulThe Ogre

A giant anthropomorphic being of the fairy tale; adversary of the hero.

Description. The Zmeu of fairy tales is distinct from the Balaur and should not be reduced to the image of a dragon. Olinescu describes it as a being with a human face but larger proportions, extraordinary strength and monstrous traits. It is one of the typical adversaries of Făt-Frumos and belongs mainly to the world of the “other realm.”

Appearance and manifestation. It has a human face, large size, a fearsome appearance and, in some descriptions, a scaly tail. It rides enchanted horses, and its characteristic weapon is the mace, which it can send ahead to announce its arrival.

Attributes / behavior / powers. It has enormous strength, can fight in fantastic forms and can transform itself. Zmei live in shining palaces separated from the human world by great waters or rivers of fire. They abduct princesses and seek to bring everything beautiful into their realm. The Zmeoaica, the maternal figure of their kind, is described as even more violent and capable of pursuing fugitives with extraordinary powers.

Associated beliefs and practices. The Zmeu belongs primarily to the structure of the heroic fairy tale: abduction, crossing into the other realm, the fight with Făt-Frumos and the freeing of the emperor’s daughter. Olinescu also emphasizes a characteristic weakness: despite its strength, the Zmeu is often foolish and can be tricked.

Alternative names / analogies: Zmeu/Smeu are spelling and regional variants. Olinescu specifies that a second meaning of Zmeu overlaps with Zburătorul; for this site, the present entry preserves the anthropomorphic fairy-tale meaning. The sources do not provide a direct external equivalent.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

15

BalaurulThe Dragon

A reptilian monster and meteorological being; the dragon of fairy tales and the dragon of the clouds.

Description. In Romanian sources, the Balaur has at least two important registers: the monster of fairy tales and the being connected with weather, clouds and Solomonari. Olinescu explicitly distinguishes between the balauri of stories and the balauri of the clouds. In both cases, the basic image is reptilian and associated with strength, danger and hard-to-reach places.

Appearance and manifestation. The fairy-tale Balaur may have one or more heads, a very large mouth, bulging eyes, nostrils that breathe fire, a long scaly body, clawed feet and an extremely powerful tail. Some have wings. It lives in forests, caves, wells or lakes.

Attributes / behavior / powers. It can swallow people and livestock, breathe fire and fight heroes. In the meteorological register, balauri rise into the clouds, are associated with thunder, lightning, rain and hail, and can be controlled by Solomonari. Pamfile records the belief that they draw water from ponds to form clouds and that “the Rainbow is the road of the Balauri.”

Associated beliefs and practices. An important tradition explains the birth of the Balaur from a snake. In Olinescu, a snake that acquires a magical stone and manages to remain for years without seeing a human can transform into a Balaur. Pamfile gives other durations and variants, showing that this genealogy is not uniform.

Alternative names / analogies: Balaur. The term can be broadly compared with the European category of the dragon, but this is only a wide analogy; Romanian sources give it distinctive functions, especially its connection with weather, water and the Solomonari. Zmeul is not synonymous with Balaurul in the anthropomorphic fairy-tale sense.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

16

SolomonarulThe Weather Sorcerer

Initiate and weather magician; master of clouds and Balauri.

Description. Solomonarul is a human figure invested with supernatural powers acquired through initiation and learning. In Olinescu’s description, future Solomonari pass through a hidden school called Solomanță, where they study and undergo difficult trials. Only those who complete their training receive the power to control clouds and Balauri.

Appearance and manifestation. In the world, he may appear as a poor, ragged and barefoot traveler carrying a bag, staff and book. The modest image contrasts with his power. In his supernatural aspect, he rides the clouds or a Balaur and directs the storm.

Attributes / behavior / powers. He can call rain, guide clouds, cause or divert hail and storms. The book of “solomonărie” and the staff are his characteristic instruments. To draw a Balaur from a lake or mountain tarn, he reads from the book and places it under a bridle; then he mounts it into the sky and guides the clouds toward the chosen place.

Associated beliefs and practices. The Solomonar may reward communities that receive him well and punish with hail those who despise him. Olinescu says that these powers are granted for a time, after which some become householders and “skillful men,” retaining the knowledge needed to stop the storms of other Solomonari.

Alternative names / analogies: Solomonar, with regional variants such as Șolomonar/Șolomariu in folkloric material. The sources used do not establish a direct external equivalent; functionally, the figure combines the initiate, the weather magician and the rider of the Balaur.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească; Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

17

SpiridușulThe Imp

A familiar spirit / magical servant with demonic origins in the traditional description.

Description. Olinescu’s Spiriduș is not merely a playful spirit, but a small spirit in the service of a master. The author describes it as a “little devil” or as the embodiment of one in a visible or invisible creature. It can bring good fortune and goods to the person who owns it, but the bond implies a dangerous relationship with the Devil.

Appearance and manifestation. Its main form is that of a tiny black little devil, “as small as a thimble.” It can also take the form of a small black chick or snake. It may be kept in a vessel and fed by its master.

Attributes / behavior / powers. It carries out the master’s commands, brings objects and goods, assists with spells and magic, can take away the productivity of cattle, bring desired people and take part in magical healing. Olinescu says that it knows and sees everything, but leads a person toward evil and the loss of the soul.

Associated beliefs and practices. It may be bought or obtained from a very small egg laid by a black hen, incubated by a person under the armpit for several weeks. A related variant is the lucky coin called “anciu” in Bukovina, believed to have a spiriduș or to be a spiriduș itself and to return continually to its owner’s purse.

Alternative names / analogies: Spiriduș; associated by Olinescu with “anciu” in the belief concerning the lucky coin. The Brehne are compared by the same author with “mischievous spiriduși,” but they form a separate category. The sources do not offer a direct external equivalent.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

18

Piaza ReaThe Evil Omen

Spirit of misfortune; personification of domestic and personal bad luck.

Description. Piaza Rea is misfortune transformed into a supernatural presence. When everything repeatedly goes wrong for a person or household, folk belief may interpret the situation as Piaza Rea having “nested” there. It is not limited to a body of its own, but may inhabit a person, animal or object.

Appearance and manifestation. It can take many forms. Olinescu mentions a person, a black dog, a hen that crows like a rooster and other domestic animals. Pamfile adds further possibilities, emphasizing the highly mobile nature of this embodiment.

Attributes / behavior / powers. Its presence brings loss of wealth, illness, the death of animals, conflict and a succession of failures. It does not act through one spectacular power, but through the accumulation of misfortunes. In this logic, a being or object becomes suspicious precisely because bad events begin with its arrival.

Associated beliefs and practices. Animals considered embodiments of Piaza Rea could be driven away or killed to stop the series of misfortunes. Olinescu opposes Piaza Rea to Piaza Bună or the “good omens,” which may be stray animals, found coins or people who bring good fortune.

Alternative names / analogies: Piaza Rea, Piaza cea Rea; its opposite is Piaza Bună. Pamfile notes similar stories about the “girl with evil omens” in Dalmatia, Sicily and among the Greeks, providing an analogy of folkloric motif rather than a single mythological equivalent.

Source: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului; Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.

Marți-searaThe Tuesday-Night Crone

Female personification of a calendrical prohibition; a punitive spirit of the day.

Description. Marți-seara is a figure that transforms the prohibition against working during a certain interval into a supernatural being capable of punishing transgression. Pamfile describes her as a malicious old woman who goes from house to house on Tuesday evening toward Wednesday, looking for women who spin, sew or continue other forbidden work.

Appearance and manifestation. She generally appears as an old woman. In stories she may deceptively present herself as a relative or familiar woman, using information about the household in order to be let inside. She has no constant monstrous anatomy; the threatening character comes from her behavior and the time of her appearance.

Attributes / behavior / powers. She can enter the house through trickery, force the victim to dance, spin or perform impossible tasks, and can punish her violently, even to death, for breaking the prohibition. Some stories show that she can be tricked by overturning objects in the house, through the help of an enchanted cat or by delaying her entry until dawn.

Associated beliefs and practices. The belief supports respect for ritual time and for the days on which certain women’s work must not be performed. Marți-seara is therefore more than a monster: she functions as the supernatural guarantor of a calendrical rule.

Alternative names / analogies: Marți-seara, Marseara, Martole, Marțolea; in some places she is also called Sfânta Miercuri. Pamfile places her in the same narrative family as other personified days, such as Miercuria and Vineria. No direct external equivalent is indicated.

Source: Tudor Pamfile, Mitologie românească. I. Dușmani și prieteni ai omului.

MătcălăulNo Direct English Equivalent

A ritual being, partly human and partly angelic.

Description. Mătcălăul is one of the figures developed less extensively in the selected sources, but precisely this rarity makes it interesting. Olinescu connects it with the holiday observed on the Monday or Tuesday after Thomas Sunday and describes it as a being “partly human, partly angelic.” It does not appear as a monster or as a punitive spirit in the strict sense.

Appearance and manifestation. It has the appearance of a handsome young man and is presented as an eternal traveler, wandering ceaselessly through the world. Ordinary people can no longer see it because they are said to have moved away from the state of purity necessary for such an encounter. Only a completely innocent person could perceive it.

Attributes / behavior / powers. The source does not attribute to it a broad repertoire of active powers. Its supernatural character results from its intermediate nature, its immortality or inaccessibility to ordinary sight, and from its connection with the sacred time of the holiday.

Associated beliefs and practices. On the day of Mătcălăul, field work is avoided for fear of storms and lightning, while women do not weave or sew. The practice of “mâtcălirea” also takes place then: women become ritual sisters or sisters of the cross, forming a ceremonial bond.

Alternative names / analogies: Mătcălăul/Mâtcălăul, depending on spelling. In the two selected sources, no direct external equivalent is indicated. Its defining element is the connection with the holiday and with ritual sisterhood.

Source: Marcel Olinescu, Mitologie românească.
